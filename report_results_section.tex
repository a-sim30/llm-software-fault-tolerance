\section{Results}

All results are produced on the mandatory image, \texttt{skimage.data.camera()},
$512\times512$, $8$-bit grayscale, converted to \texttt{float64} in $[0,1]$.
The complete notebook executes in $\approx\Truntime$\,s from a fresh Colab
runtime, against the three-minute limit.

\subsection{Invariance study}

Because the method claims to be a \emph{scale invariant} feature transform,
invariance is measured rather than asserted. A warp with a \emph{known}
ground-truth transform is applied to Cameraman, the entire pipeline is re-run
on the warped image, and the two keypoint sets are compared.

\paragraph{Protocol.} Only the \emph{common region} is used: the part of the
warped image that genuinely originates from the original one. The validity
mask is eroded by $24$\,px so that a keypoint's whole support lies inside it,
which excludes the black corners a rotation introduces. All warping uses
bilinear interpolation with anti-aliasing disabled, since
\texttt{scikit-image}'s anti-aliasing filter is itself Gaussian and would
contradict the premise of the method. Three quantities are reported.

\begin{itemize}\itemsep2pt
\item \textbf{Repeatability} --- a purely geometric detector score: the
fraction of common-region keypoints re-detected within $2.5$\,px of their
mapped position and within a factor $1.5$ of their expected scale. It involves
no descriptor at all.
\item \textbf{Match precision} --- the fraction of accepted descriptor matches
that are geometrically correct within $3$\,px.
\item \textbf{Matching score} --- correct matches divided by the number of
keypoints available in the common region, $\min(n_A,n_B)$.
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{Matching rule.} Lowe's ratio test at $0.8$ is used with two
modifications. First, the second nearest neighbour must lie at least $4$\,px
away from the first: without this, a structure detected twice (two
orientations, or two adjacent scales) acts as its own second neighbour, the
ratio approaches $1$, and a perfectly good match is discarded. Second, matches
must be mutual nearest neighbours.

\paragraph{Rotation.} Across $15^{\circ}$, $30^{\circ}$ and $45^{\circ}$ the
figures are essentially flat --- repeatability $53$--$56\,\%$ and precision
$83$--$85\,\%$ --- with no systematic dependence on the angle. That flatness is
the practical payoff of the isotropic nine-point stencil of
Section~\ref{sec:ss}: the scale space itself, and therefore the set of
detected keypoints, does not favour the axes of the pixel grid.

\paragraph{The $90^{\circ}$ row is a self-diagnostic.} A rotation by
$90^{\circ}$ about the centre maps pixel $(x,y)$ to $(511-y,\,x)$: integer to
integer, with no interpolation whatsoever. Since the stencil and
$\det\mathbf{H}$ are both $90^{\circ}$ symmetric, an ideal implementation would
score $100\,\%$. The measured $81.3\,\%$, and the fact that $n_A\neq n_B$,
localise the residual error precisely --- and the cause is the filter-free
decimation. Octave~$1$ retains the pixels with $x$ and $y$ both even; in the
rotated image those are $x'=511-y$ and $y'=x$ both even, i.e.\ $x$ even and $y$
\emph{odd} in original coordinates. The two pyramids therefore sample
\emph{different lattices} from octave~$1$ onwards. Octave~$0$ repeats almost
perfectly, the coarser octaves do not, and the weighted average lands near
$80\,\%$. This is exactly the aliasing that SIFT's Gaussian pre-blur prevents,
and it is the concrete price of eliminating that fifth Gaussian; the optional
$3\times3$ box average described in Section~\ref{sec:ss} is the Gaussian-free
remedy.

\paragraph{Zoom.} The two zoom rows are not directly comparable to each other,
because the $2.5$\,px tolerance is applied in the \emph{warped} frame: at
$\times1.50$ that corresponds to only $1.67$ reference pixels, whereas at
$\times0.75$ it corresponds to $3.33$. Zooming in is therefore measured under a
stricter criterion than zooming out, which accounts for most of the gap between
$38.7\,\%$ and $81.2\,\%$, and the $38.7\,\%$ figure should not be read as the
detector performing twice as badly. Two genuine effects remain. Up-sampling to
$768\times768$ resolves fine structure that does not exist at the original
resolution, so the warped image yields far more keypoints ($3976$ against
$1044$) and the extra detections have no counterpart to match. Down-sampling
loses every structure finer than $\sigma_0=1.6$\,px, since --- unlike Lowe's
implementation --- the input image is never doubled before processing.

\paragraph{Overall.} Precision stays in the $72$--$96\,\%$ band across every
test, so when the detector does re-find a structure, the Gaussian-free
descriptor identifies it correctly the large majority of the time. The
detector, not the descriptor, is the limiting component, and the $90^{\circ}$
analysis above says exactly which part of it.
